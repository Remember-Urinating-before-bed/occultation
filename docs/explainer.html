A learning project · Hong Kong

The Moon
blots out a star

That is all this project is about — and everything it takes to predict it. No astronomy background needed. Start here.

1What is an occultation?

One object passes in front of another and hides it. That is the whole idea.

Every month

Not rare

The Moon circles the sky once a month and passes in front of many stars. From any one city, some happen every week.

2Why would anyone compute this?

Because the answer is a time, and a time is easy to check.

The question

“From Hong Kong, at what time does the star disappear — and does it happen at all?”

That is what this program answers. One place, one star, one moment.

The catch

You cannot look it up for a star nobody has published.

So the goal is to compute it yourself, from the same textbook formulas observatories use — and to prove the answer is right.

How you prove it: a famous textbook (Jean Meeus, Astronomical Tables) contains a fully worked example — the star Regulus, on 1 March 1999, seen from Palomar Mountain. The book prints the answer. This project reproduces that answer to five decimal places, and that reproduction is a permanent automated test.

3How the calculation works

Four ideas, in order. No formulas needed to follow along.

Idea 1

Flatten the sky onto a sheet of paper

Imagine a giant flat sheet standing between the Earth and the Moon, at right angles to the Moon's shadow. Everything is projected onto that sheet. The Moon becomes a circle; the star becomes a dot.

The book calls this the fundamental plane.

Idea 2

Describe the Moon's shadow with six numbers

On that sheet, where is the Moon's shadow centre, and how fast is it sliding? Three numbers describe the horizontal position, three the vertical. They are called Besselian elements.

This project takes them as given input — a later step will compute them.

Idea 3

Turn “where you stand” into the same sheet

Your latitude, longitude and height become a point on the same sheet. Now the star, the Moon and you are all in one picture, and “is the star hidden?” is just “is the dot inside the circle?”

Idea 4

Slide time until they are closest

Guess a time, measure the gap, jump to the moment the gap is smallest, repeat until the jump is tiny. Four rounds is enough. The smallest gap is the answer.

This is called the closest approach.

Blink

Very fast

The star does not fade. It goes out instantly and comes back instantly — usually within a minute or two.

Where you stand

Depends on you

Move a few kilometres and the star may miss the Moon completely. That is why the answer is per-location.

4What the program actually prints

Seven numbers and a yes/no. That is a complete answer.

WhatMeaning in plain words
hours_after_referenceWhen it happens, counted from the reference moment in the tables.
universal_time_hourThe same moment on a world clock.
separation_in_moon_radiiThe gap between star and Moon centre, measured in Moon-widths. Under 1 means hidden.
limb_clearance_in_moon_radiiHow far past the Moon's edge the star passes. Negative means it went behind.
position_angle_degWhere on the Moon's edge the star disappears — like a clock face.
altitude_degHow high the star is above your horizon. Below zero, you cannot see it.
is_occultationThe yes/no answer for your location.
The worked example

Regulus, 1 March 1999, Palomar

The book's own example, and this project's test case:

  • gap: 1.1526 Moon-widths
  • clearance: +0.1526 Moon-widths
  • angle: 24.67°
  • altitude: +43° (visible)
  • hidden? No — it misses by about 2 arcminutes
Honest limits

What this version does not do yet

  • It does not compute the Besselian elements — you supply them.
  • It gives the closest approach, not the exact start and end of the hiding.
  • It assumes the Moon is a smooth ball; real craters shift the edge.
  • It is not observation-grade. Do not plan a telescope night with it.

7Five more questions, answered

Each one starts from the sentence that confused you, then draws it. Jump to one: A1 · A2 · A3 · A4 · A5

A1 · from Scenario 1

“Stand on the star and look back” — where am I, and where is the Earth?

You are standing on the star, so the whole sky is behind your back. The Earth is in front of you, far away.

That is exactly right, and it is not a contradiction — it is the point. You have not moved to the Moon, and you have not moved to the Earth. You have moved to the star, which is the one thing in the picture that never moves.

Why bother? Because from your chair on Earth you cannot see the Moon move. The Moon takes about 50 minutes to travel its own width, so during the minute or two the star is hidden, the Moon looks frozen. From the star, though, the Moon is the thing that moves and the Earth is a big blue target it slides across. So you “change seats” to the place where the motion is visible.

“Like a torch beam.” The Moon is lit by the Sun, so it casts a shadow. That shadow is a long thin cone, and the line down the middle of it is the shadow axis — the beam. From the star you see that beam come straight at you, so on the picture it is a straight line crossing the Earth's disc.

Where is the Sun in this picture?
Off to one side, lighting the Moon. The beam points away from the Sun, so it is not aimed at the star — it just happens to sweep across the Earth's disc while you watch.
Is the star really that close to the Earth?
No. The star is effectively infinitely far away, which is why the beam looks parallel to your line of sight and why the Earth appears as a flat disc rather than a ball. That is a convenience, not a lie: it makes the picture 2-D.
A2 · from Scenario 6

What is P?

P is a clock face on the Moon. It says which direction, from the Moon's centre, the star is — and therefore where on the Moon's edge the star disappears.

It is measured as an angle from the Moon's North point, turning towards East: 0° = straight up, 90° = right, 180° = straight down, 270° = left. The book prints it in degrees; the code returns the same number as position_angle_deg.

Why does the book care about P?
Because “the star disappears at 24.7°” is something an observer can use: it says which side of the Moon to watch, and it is the number you compare against a photograph.
Why does the drawing look steeper than 24.7°?
The picture is exaggerated so the gap is visible. The real value is nearly “up, and a little to the right”; the regression test pins it at P = 24.6654° for Regulus on 1 March 1999.
A3 · from Scenario 1

Why does |γ| > 1.270 mean nobody on Earth sees the occultation?

Because γ is the distance from the Earth's centre to the shadow beam, measured in Earth radii — and 1.270 is as far as the beam can be and still clip the Earth.

Read the number 1.270 as 1 Earth radius + 0.270 of another one. The extra 0.270 is not decoration: it is the Moon's shadow radius, which is about 0.27 Earth radii. So 1.270 means “the beam's centre line is exactly far enough that the beam's edge just grazes the Earth's edge”. Beyond that, the whole beam misses.

Where 1.270 comes from, in one line: Earth's radius (1) + the Moon's shadow radius (≈0.27). It is a cheap filter: before any trigonometry, one number tells the book “this event is visible somewhere on Earth” or “skip it”.

Why is γ measured in Earth radii rather than kilometres?
Because then the Earth's edge is always at 1 and the Moon's shadow is always 0.27 — no unit conversions, and “does it hit?” is a comparison against 1.270.
Is 1.270 exact?
It is the book's working limit for occultations, printed as |γ| < 1.270. It is a filter, not a precision result; the real answer comes from the full calculation.
A4 · from Scenario 3

x = X0 + X1·t + X2·t² — what is each symbol, and why is there a t²?

X0 is where the Moon's dot is now. X1·t is how far it has slid. X2·t² is a small correction for the fact that the slide is not perfectly straight.

SymbolNameMeaningUnits
xthe dot's east–west positionWhere the Moon's shadow pokes through the sheet, measured along the x-axisEarth radii
X0position at the reference instant“Where the dot is at t = 0.” A single number from the book's tableEarth radii
X1speedHow many Earth radii the dot slides per hour. The Moon's motion, almost constantEarth radii / hour
ttimeHours after the reference instant. Negative before it, positive afterhours
X2curvatureA tiny correction applied to t² — the “slightly bent” part of the pathEarth radii / hour²

There is an identical equation for y (north–south), with Y0, Y1, Y2. Together those six numbers are the Besselian elements: they pin down the dot and its motion completely.

Why is t² the right fix, and not something bigger?
Because over the few hours an occultation lasts, the curvature is tiny. A parabola (a t² term) is the cheapest curve that can bend, so one extra number buys all the accuracy needed. Adding t³ would be inventing precision the tables do not have.
How big is the bend, really?
Small enough that the straight-line model gets you close, and large enough that the final digits of the answer depend on it. That is exactly why X2 exists: it is the difference between “roughly right” and “matches the book”.
Where does the code use this?
FundamentalPlanePolynomial.value_at(t) evaluates it, and rate_at(t) evaluates its slope X1 + 2·X2·t — used by the solver to know how fast the gap is changing.
A5 · from Scenario 3

“The Moon moves like a bridge across the sheet” — what does that mean?

The sheet is a fixed map. The Moon is not on the map — it is a moving thing whose shadow touches the map at one point that keeps moving. That moving contact point is what “the Moon” means on the sheet.

Two pictures, and the difference matters:

  • The wrong picture: the Moon is a sticker lying on the sheet, sliding around on it like a puck. Then the sheet would be moving with it, and the Earth's centre O would be sliding too — nothing would be fixed, and the geometry would be useless.
  • The right picture: the sheet is nailed to the Earth's centre and never moves. The Moon hangs above it and casts a dot onto it, the way a lamp hanging from a ceiling casts a dot on the floor as you carry it. The dot crosses the sheet; the sheet stays still.

So “bridge” is a good word for the track: the dot enters the sheet at one edge, spans it, and leaves at the other — like a bridge across a river. The track is drawn on the sheet, but the Moon is the thing doing the moving.

Then what does the circle around the dot mean?
It is the Moon's outline drawn at the dot's position: radius k = 0.272495 Earth radii. The dot is the Moon's centre; the circle is its edge. “Is the star hidden?” becomes “is the star's dot inside this circle?”
Why is the sheet nailed to O?
Because then the Earth's centre is the origin of the coordinates, so every distance is directly comparable with the Earth's radius — which is why γ and k are both in Earth radii.
