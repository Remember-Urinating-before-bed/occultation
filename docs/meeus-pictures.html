0What is in that folder?

A scan of a book, and a machine's attempt to read it out loud.

The book

MeeusTables_Occultation.pdf

Jean Meeus, Astronomical Tables, the Occultations chapter. It is a photocopy — 128 pictures of pages, no text inside. Printed pages 219–346.

It is copyrighted, so it can never be committed to the repository. It lives in local/, which Git ignores completely.

The machine reading

all_pages.txt

Apple's Vision OCR walked over the 128 page-images and typed out whatever it thought it saw. That is all_pages.txt; ocr.swift is the 50-line script that did it.

It is a search index, not a source of truth. It is how you find "the words fundamental plane are on page 221" — then you open the PDF and read the page with your own eyes.

Why the distinction matters. The OCR turns sin φ′ into sin c', drops degree signs, and scrambles every multi-column table. A number copied out of all_pages.txt into code would be a guess. So the project's rule is: read every number off the page image by eye, and write down both the printed page and the PDF page.

So what is this folder for? It is the answer key. The book prints a complete worked example (Regulus, 1 March 1999, Palomar Mountain) with every intermediate number. The project computes the same thing and checks it digit by digit. That is why the folder exists at all.

1The whole book in one sentence

A star is a fixed pinprick. The Moon slides past it. Work out whether the Moon's edge crosses the pinprick — and when.

The only question the whole chapter answers: does the Moon's edge cross the star, as seen from your chair?

Everything else — the tables, the Greek letters, the trigonometry — exists to answer that one question accurately, and to answer it before you go outside.

2The six scenarios, drawn

This is the order the chapter builds them in. Each picture is one page of the book.

Scenario 1 · printed p. 220

Stand on the star and look back

You cannot see the Moon move. So change seats: watch the Moon from the star.

From there, the Earth is a flat blue disc and the Moon is a little circle crawling across it. The Moon drags a long thin shadow behind it, like a torch beam. That beam is a straight line: the shadow axis.

The distance from the Earth's centre O to the nearest point of that line is called γ (gamma). It is measured in Earth radii — the Earth's own width is the ruler.

Why think this way?
Because the Moon's motion and the Earth's rotation then become two simple motions in one flat picture, instead of a complicated 3-D tangle. A1 explains the seat-swap and the torch beam.
What the book says about γ
If |γ| is bigger than 1.270, the shadow misses the Earth entirely and nobody on the planet sees the occultation. That is a fast global filter. A3 draws the three cases and shows where 1.270 comes from.
Scenario 2 · printed p. 221

Put a sheet of glass in the way

A 3-D problem becomes a 2-D problem. That is the entire trick.

Hold a pane of glass at right angles to the shadow axis, passing through the Earth's centre. The Moon's shadow pokes through the glass at one point. Mark it. That point is "the Moon", as far as the maths is concerned.

Draw two lines on the glass: x pointing towards the equinox, y pointing north. Now the Moon is just a dot at coordinates (x, y), with a circle around it of radius k = 0.272495 — the Moon's size measured in Earth radii.

Why a sheet?
Because on a flat sheet, "is the dot inside the circle?" is a distance comparison, not a 3-D geometry problem. That is where Pythagoras comes in (see §3).
Why that sheet?
Because it is perpendicular to the shadow, the shadow never smears across it — it stays a clean circle of constant radius. Any other sheet would give you an ellipse and a headache.
Is the Moon lying on this sheet?
No — the sheet is nailed to the Earth's centre and the Moon hangs above it, casting a dot. See A5 for the two pictures side by side.
Scenario 3 · printed p. 221

Six numbers describe the Moon

Where it is, how fast it slides, and a small correction for the curve.

The book's tables give you, for each occultation, six numbers. Three say where the shadow dot is at the reference instant, three say how it slides across the sheet.

Two of them are just "position now": x = X0 + X1·t + X2·t². Read it as: start here, add the distance travelled, add a tiny bend for the fact that the path curves.

Why a squared term?
The Moon's path is not a perfectly straight ruler line across the sheet — it curves very slightly. t² is the cheapest way to say "slightly curved". A4 names every symbol and draws the bend.
What the project does with them
Nothing yet. It takes the six numbers as given input. Computing them from an ephemeris is a later milestone. That is why this whole work-package needs no data downloads at all.
Scenario 4 · printed p. 224

You are a dot too

The Earth spins you around, so where you are depends on the time. That is why the answer is per-place and per-minute.

Your latitude tells the maths how far north you are; your longitude tells it how far around the spin you are; your height adds a whisker. Those three become ρ sin φ′ and ρ cos φ′ — the two numbers that place you inside the Earth.

Then a rotation turns them into ξ and η (xi and eta): your dot on the flat sheet, in the same units as the Moon's dot.

Why the squashed-Earth correction?
The Earth is not a sphere — it bulges at the equator. k′ = 0.99664719 is the book's way of saying "nudge your latitude a little before using it".
Why does height matter?
Because being 1706 m up at Palomar shifts your dot sideways by a tiny amount — enough to matter when the whole question is "did it miss by 0.15 of a Moon width?"
Scenario 5 · printed pp. 225–226

Slide time until the gap stops shrinking

You cannot solve it in one step, so you nudge it until it stops moving. That is all "iteration" means.

Start with a guess (t = 0). Work out where the star is relative to the Moon at that moment, and how fast that gap is changing. Geometry then tells you exactly how far forward or backward to jump to reach the smallest gap. Jump. Re-measure. Repeat.

When the jump is smaller than a millionth of an hour — about 3.6 milliseconds — you are done. The book says "two or three iterations will suffice"; starting from t = 0 the project needs four.

Why does jumping work?
Near the closest approach the gap behaves like a simple U-shaped curve, and the bottom of a U can be found from its slope. That is τ = −(u·u′ + v·v′)/n² — see §4.
Why not just try every minute?
You could, but the jump lands on the answer to six decimals in four steps. The book's method is the fast, exact version of "try every minute".
Scenario 6 · printed pp. 225, 229

The verdict, and the book's own example

Regulus, 1 March 1999, Palomar Mountain: the book's answer is no occultation — it misses by about 2 arcminutes.

The book prints every intermediate number, so it is a perfect answer key. The project reproduces all of them.

Why "no occultation" is still a useful answer
Because it tells you the star came close — 0.15 Moon-widths from the edge. Move a few hundred kilometres and it would be hidden. That is exactly the "depends where you stand" idea.
Why the tolerances are not tighter
The book prints Δ to 4 decimals and h to whole degrees. The test checks to the precision the source actually supports — never tighter. Tightening it would be inventing accuracy.

3Now the maths — and why it is only these three

Pythagoras measures a gap. sin and cos turn angles into distances. tan turns distances back into an angle. That is the whole toolbox.

The one idea behind all of it: on a flat sheet, every question is either "how far apart are these two dots?" or "which way is that dot from this one?" The first is Pythagoras. The second is tan. sin and cos are the bridge that puts a direction (an angle on the sky) into coordinates (a dot on the sheet).

Tool 1 · Pythagoras

Pythagoras is just a ruler for diagonal gaps

You know the gap sideways and the gap up-and-down. You want the gap as the crow flies.

That is literally what Pythagoras' theorem does. Walk 3 blocks east and 4 blocks north and you are 5 blocks from where you started — not 7. The same thing happens on the sheet: the sideways gap is u, the up-down gap is v, and the true gap is the diagonal.

In the chapter it shows up as n² = u′² + v′²: not the gap itself, but the speed — how fast the gap is changing, made of a sideways speed and an up-down speed.

Why is the triangle always right-angled?
Because u is measured along the x-axis and v along the y-axis, and those two axes are perpendicular by construction. Right angle guaranteed — so Pythagoras always applies.
Where you can see it in the code
math.hypot(x_rate, y_rate) in core/local_circumstances.py — that is Pythagoras, spelled out.
Tool 2 · sin and cos

sin and cos are shadows of a stick

Astronomy gives you angles. The sheet needs distances. sin and cos do the conversion — nothing more mysterious than that.

Imagine a stick of length 1, pointed in some direction. Its shadow on the floor tells you how far it reaches sideways (cos); its shadow on the wall tells you how far it reaches upward (sin). Tilt the stick and both shadows change — but the stick is always length 1.

That is why the chapter is full of them. ρ cos φ′ sin H means: start with your distance from the Earth's axis, tilt it by your hour angle, take the sideways shadow. It is a shadow, not a spell.

Why does sin h = sin d sin φ + cos d cos φ cos H work?
It is the same shadow trick applied twice and added up. sin d sin φ is the part of the star's height that comes from the star's declination; cos d cos φ cos H is the part that comes from the Earth's spin carrying you around. Add the two contributions and you get the altitude. That is formula (8) — the "is it above my horizon?" test.
Where you can see it in the code
_star_altitude_deg() is exactly that one line, in core/local_circumstances.py.
Tool 3 · tan and atan2

tan turns two gaps back into a direction

You already have the sideways gap and the up-down gap. tan converts that pair into "which way?".

Pythagoras went gaps → distance. tan goes the other way: two gaps → angle. In the chapter it answers a very human question: where on the Moon's edge does the star vanish? That is the position angle P, measured from the Moon's North point, going round towards East.

The book writes tan P = u / v, with a careful note about which quadrant you are in. The code does that with one call to atan2, which handles all four quadrants automatically — that is why the code looks slightly different from the printed formula but gives the same answer.

Why can a ratio give an angle?
Because in a right triangle the ratio of the two short sides determines the shape. Same ratio, same angle — always. tan is just the name of that ratio.
Why "from the North point", not "from the north pole"?
Because the Moon's face is tilted in the sky. Its own "up" is not the sky's up. P is measured on the Moon's face, the way you would point at a spot on a clock. A2 draws it as a clock hand.

4Why the whole thing works at all

Three reasons, none of them complicated.

Reason 1

A star is a dot

It is so far away that it has no size and does not move measurably during a few minutes. So the whole problem is one moving circle against one fixed dot. That is why the book can write D1 = 0 and H1 = 15.04107 and be right.

Reason 2

Two motions, both simple

The Moon slides across the sheet at a nearly constant speed; the Earth spins you around at a constant rate. Add them and the relative gap is a straight line — so its closest point can be computed, not searched for.

Reason 3

Everything is in Earth radii

Distances are measured in Earth widths, and the Moon's radius is a fixed fraction k = 0.272495. So "is it hidden?" is just "is the gap less than 1?" — no unit conversions, no scale factors.

And the reason the project can be checked at all: the book prints a full worked example with every intermediate number. If the code gets t = 0.455608 and Δ = 1.1526 for Regulus 1999, the implementation is right. That is the entire purpose of local/meeus_ocr: not to teach the maths, but to be the answer key the maths is graded against.

5What this book does not cover

Honest limits, so you do not expect more than the chapter gives.

Not in this chapter

What the chapter leaves out

  • It does not compute the Besselian elements — it prints them in tables. Producing them from an ephemeris is a different job.
  • The exact immersion and emersion times are a later section (printed p. 226). The project has not implemented them yet.
  • The Moon is treated as a smooth ball. Real craters and mountains shift the edge by a second or two of arc — which matters a lot for grazing events.
  • It says nothing about whether the sky is dark enough to actually see the event.
