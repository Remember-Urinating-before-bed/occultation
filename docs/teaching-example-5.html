1The one question the whole example answers

A star is a fixed pinprick. The Moon slides past it. Did the Moon's edge cross the pinprick — and when?

On 1999 March 1, seen from Palomar Mountain Observatory, how close did the Moon's centre come to the star Regulus — and did the Moon actually cover it?

The answer turns out to be no: Regulus passed about 0.15 lunar radii — roughly 2 arc-minutes — north of the Moon's edge. But you cannot know that until you have computed the closest approach. That computation is Example 5.

Words you will meet. An occultation is a star being hidden by the Moon — a tiny eclipse of a star. Regulus is a bright star. Palomar Mountain is the observatory site. Dynamical Time (TD) is the astronomer's uniform clock; Universal Time (UT) is the everyday clock, and they differ by ΔT (here 65 seconds).

2The one picture to hold in your head

Change seats: stand on the star and look back at the Earth.

The blue dashed line in the picture is the fundamental plane: a flat sheet through the Earth's centre O, at right angles to the shadow axis. Two things to keep separate:

  • The plane itself is fixed. The axis points in one direction for the whole event, so the plane's tilt is set once and never changes. Finding the plane needs only O plus the axis direction — not the sliding point.
  • What moves is a point on the plane. The Moon's shadow point (x, y) — the green dot — slides across this fixed sheet as time passes. That is the "slides across the disk" arrow: it locates the Moon's point, not the plane.

Once we are on that fixed plane, everything becomes flat and simple:

  • the Moon's shadow beam is a moving point (x, y);
  • your observing site is another point (ξ, η);
  • the star sits still.

The whole chapter is then: where is the Moon-point, where is the observer-point, and how far apart are they? All distances are in Earth equatorial radii (the Earth's own radius is the ruler), and time is in hours of Dynamical Time measured from a reference instant called To.

3Step 1 — the Earth is squashed, so fix your latitude

The first formula in the book exists only because the Earth is not a sphere.

Step 1 · printed p. 224

Why a correction is needed at all

Your GPS latitude is measured from a plumb line, not from the Earth's centre.

φ versus φ′ — same kind of thing, different reference line. Both are angles in degrees, and both measure how far north you are. The only difference is the line they are measured from:

Measured from…CalledValue at Palomar
φthe plumb line (local "down")geographic latitude33.3562°
φ′the line from the centre O to yougeocentric latitude33.2679°

So φ is what your GPS shows; φ′ is the same idea but measured from the Earth's centre. They differ by φ − φ′ ≈ 0.0883° at Palomar — about 0.09°, or roughly 1/11 of a degree.

What is a plumb line? Imagine tying a small weight (a "plumb bob") to a string and holding the string up. The string hangs straight down. That hanging string is a plumb line — it points in the direction of "down" at your spot. It is the same direction a spirit level or a builder's vertical uses.

Does it point at the centre? No — and this is true even if the Earth were a perfect sphere. A plumb line points at the Earth's centre only at the North Pole and the South Pole. Everywhere else (Palomar included) it misses the centre and passes to one side of it. You can see this on any globe: hang a weight at Hong Kong and the string points at the centre of the globe, not at the point directly below you.

So what does the squashed shape add? It adds a second, extra gap. On a perfect sphere the plumb line still misses the centre, but the angle between "down" and "to the centre" stays small and simple. On the real squashed Earth the ground is tilted, so the plumb line is pushed a little further away from the centre line. The two effects add up, and the book's formula k′ = 0.99664719 is exactly the correction for the squashed part.

The shadow geometry is all "as seen from the centre", so we need the geocentric latitude φ′ (measured from the centre) instead of the geographic one φ (measured from the plumb line).

Plumb line vs centre line
Red line = the plumb line (points "down", misses the centre). Green line = the line from the centre to you. The two lines are not the same, and that small gap is the whole reason Step 1 exists.
Only at the poles do they agree
At the North Pole and South Pole the plumb line does point at the centre, so φ = φ′ there. Everywhere else they differ — and the squashed Earth makes the difference a little bigger.
The squash factor
k′ = 0.99664719 is 1 − flattening. If the Earth were a perfect sphere this would be exactly 1 and the squashed part of the correction would vanish (but the plumb line would still miss the centre).
Why two products, not one angle
The later trigonometry only ever needs ρ sin φ′ and ρ cos φ′ — never φ′ or ρ on their own. So we compute the products directly.

The formulas

# Meeus, printed p. 224. k' = 0.99664719, a = 6378140 m.
tan ω′ = k′ · tan φ
ρ sin φ′ = k′ · sin ω′ + (h / a) · sin φ
ρ cos φ′ =        cos ω′ + (h / a) · cos φ

What are ω and ω′? They are helper angles — a "reduced latitude" pair. They are not physical directions you could point at; they exist only so the arithmetic comes out right.

  • ω is the book's own symbol for the reduced latitude. The book defines it by tan ω = k′ · tan φ, then uses ω inside the two ρ sin φ′ / ρ cos φ′ formulas.
  • ω′ is the same helper angle, just renamed in this walkthrough (a prime is added to match the φ′ style). The two are the same number: ω′ = ω.
  • It is close to your latitude φ but a little smaller (for Palomar, 33.2679° versus 33.3562°). That small shrink is the squash being folded in.
  • You never use ω′ on its own — it is only a stepping stone to the two products the chapter really needs.

So: ω and ω′ are the same auxiliary angle; the book calls it ω, this walkthrough calls it ω′.

But why do we need a helper angle at all? Because the formula has to start from φ (what your GPS gives you) and end at φ′ (what the geometry needs) — and tan φ′ = k′ · tan φ cannot be solved for φ′ in closed form. The trick is to split that one unsolvable step into two solvable ones by inventing ω:

# the trick: route through omega instead of solving tan(phi') = k' tan(phi) directly
tan ω = k′ · tan φ          # easy: φ in, ω out
tan φ′ = tan ω / k′         # easy: ω in, φ′ out

Both steps are ordinary arctan calls. You never have to invert the squashed relationship in one go — ω is the convenient halfway house that makes each step trivial. That is the whole job of a helper angle.

Where does tan ω = k′ · tan φ come from? It is not a new law — it is the definition of ω. The book wants the geocentric latitude φ′, and the squashed-Earth relation for it is

tan φ′ = k′ · tan φ          # the one relation we actually need

That relation is fine as a statement, but awkward to use: you want φ′ itself, and there is no tidy inverse. So the book simply gives the whole right-hand side a name:

# step 1 — name the thing we keep having to compute
tan ω = k′ · tan φ           # omega := arctan(k' tan phi)   (just a rename!)

# step 2 — but that name IS tan phi', because of the relation above
tan φ′ = k′ · tan φ = tan ω
# so:   φ′ = ω   (same quadrant, both between -90 and +90)

That is the entire trick: ω is not a separate quantity, it is literally the same number as φ′, introduced as a name for the intermediate arctan(k′ tan φ). Nothing "cancels" — the two are equal by construction.

Check it with Palomar's numbers:

# phi = 33.3562 deg, k' = 0.99664719
tan φ        = 0.658282
k′ · tan φ   = 0.655811
arctan(...)  = 33.2679°   =  ω   =  φ′   ✓  (book prints φ′ = 33.2679°)

Why bother, if ω = φ′? Because the two ρ formulas are built from sin ω and cos ω — and writing them with the named angle makes the next step (the k′ squash in the sin line) obvious. The name is a bookkeeping device so the algebra reads cleanly.

Where those two formulas come from

You do not have to trust them — they are just "the height and the reach" of a point, written out. Work in the flat slice that contains O, the equator, and you. Draw a right-angled triangle from O out to your true position P:

  • the hypotenuse is OP, whose length is ρ (in Earth radii);
  • the angle at O is φ′;
  • so the vertical side (height above the equator) is ρ sin φ′, and the horizontal side (reach along the equator) is ρ cos φ′.
# the geometry, split into the two pieces the book adds up
ρ sin φ′ = (height of the point on the surface)  +  (height gained by h)
ρ cos φ′ = (reach  of the point on the surface)  +  (reach  gained by h)

Piece 1 — the surface point (at sea level). On the squashed Earth the surface point is not at angle φ from the centre; it sits at the reduced latitude ω′. Its two coordinates are k′ sin ω′ and cos ω′ — the k′ is the squash shrinking the vertical side. That is exactly the first term of each formula.

Piece 2 — the extra bit from your height h. Going up by h metres moves you along the plumb line, which is tilted at the geographic latitude φ. In units of Earth radii that height is h / a, and its two components are (h / a) sin φ and (h / a) cos φ — the second term of each formula.

Add the two pieces and you get the formulas exactly:

# piece 1 (surface)  +  piece 2 (height)
ρ sin φ′ = k′ · sin ω′  +  (h / a) · sin φ
ρ cos φ′ =      cos ω′  +  (h / a) · cos φ

Why ω′ and not φ in piece 1? Because the surface point is measured from the centre (that is what "geocentric" means), and from the centre the squashed surface sits at ω′. The k′ then squashes the vertical side to match. Piece 2 uses φ because your height is measured along the plumb line, which is what φ describes.

Why does k′ appear in the sin line but not the cos line? Because the squash only shrinks the vertical (north–south) direction, never the horizontal (equatorial) one.

  • The equator keeps its full radius a, so the horizontal reach cos ω′ is unchanged — no k′.
  • The poles are pulled in by the factor k′ = 0.9966…, so the vertical height sin ω′ is multiplied by k′ — that is the k′.
  • Same reason the height term (h / a) sin φ is untouched by k′: the h part is a plain offset added on top, not part of the squashed surface shape.

In one line: k′ is the squash of the vertical only, so it can only ever appear next to a sin.

Which symbols are known, and which are the unknowns

First, a warning that trips everyone up: in this chapter φ and φ′ are angles (degrees), while ρ, a and h are distances. The confusing part is that the same Greek letter ρ is used for two different distances — see the note under the picture.

Angles vs distances — and the two meanings of ρ.

  • φ and φ′ are angles (degrees).
  • a and h are distances in metres (6378140 m and 1706 m).
  • ρ is a distance, but the book uses the same letter for two different ones:
    • the actual distance from O to you, in metres — that is a plus a tiny bit for your height (about 6 378 000 m);
    • the same distance divided by the Earth's radius a, i.e. measured in Earth radii. That is the ρ in the formulas, and for Palomar it is 0.9967.

So: yes — the ρ used in the formulas is (your true distance from the centre) ÷ (Earth's equatorial radius a). It comes out close to 1 because you stand almost exactly one Earth radius from the centre. The picture's green line has length ρ measured that way.

Plugging in Example 5's numbers

# φ = 33.3562°, h = 1706 m
tan ω′ = 0.99664719 × tan 33.3562° = 0.99664719 × 0.658155 = 0.655948
ω′     = arctan(0.655948) = 33.2679°      # a hair less than 33.3562°, as promised

ρ sin φ′ = 0.99664719 × sin 33.2679° + (1706/6378140) × sin 33.3562°
         = 0.99664719 × 0.548600     + 0.00026747     × 0.549773
         = 0.546862   # book prints +0.546862 ✓

ρ cos φ′ = cos 33.2679°             + (1706/6378140) × cos 33.3562°
         = 0.836072                 + 0.00026747     × 0.835314
         = 0.836338   # book prints +0.836338 ✓

Both products match the book to the last printed digit. That is the whole of Step 1.

4Step 2 — one pass: where are the two points?

Guess a time, then compute the star's position relative to the Moon, and the speed of that motion.

Start with the book's first guess t = 0 (i.e. exactly at the reference instant To). At any trial time t we compute:

  • the Moon's shadow point (x, y) — from the Besselian polynomials;
  • the observer's point (ξ, η) — from the Step-1 products and the hour angle H;
  • their difference, the relative position (u, v), and its hourly rate (u′, v′).
# Meeus, printed pp. 224-225. For a star: d = d0 (declination is constant).
H   = H0 + H1·t + λ_east − ΔT/239.345
x   = X0 + X1·t + X2·t²      x′ = X1 + 2·X2·t
y   = Y0 + Y1·t + Y2·t²      y′ = Y1 + 2·Y2·t
ξ   = ρ cos φ′ · sin H
η   = ρ sin φ′ · cos d − ρ cos φ′ · cos H · sin d
ξ′  = 0.01745329 · H1 · ρ cos φ′ · cos H
η′  = 0.01745329 · H1 · ξ · sin d
u   = x − ξ        v  = y − η
u′  = x′ − ξ′      v′ = y′ − η′
n²  = u′² + v′²

Two sign traps. The book's λ is west-positive, but this project stores east-positive longitude, so −λ becomes +λ_east. And ΔT is divided by 239.345 (seconds of time per degree of hour angle), not by 3600.

What every symbol means

Each line above is one small job. Read them in this order — time first, then the Moon, then you, then the gap between you.

Why the Moon's terms look like polynomials. The Moon moves almost in a straight line across the plane over the few hours that matter, so x and y are written as value + slope·t + curvature·t². The X2·t² term is the gentle bend. Differentiating that polynomial is why x′ = X1 + 2·X2·t — the 2 comes straight from the power rule.

5Step 3 — "jump to the instant of closest approach"

This is the heart of the method, and it is a genuine aha.

Step 3 · printed p. 225

When is the star closest to the Moon?

Think of a car driving past you. It is closest at the instant it is exactly beside you — when its velocity is at right angles to your line of sight.

Here the "car" is the Moon's relative position (u, v) and its "velocity" is (u′, v′). Closest approach is when those two vectors are perpendicular, i.e. their dot product is zero: u·u′ + v·v′ = 0.

We are not there yet, so the dot product is not zero. Because the motion is straight and at constant speed, the exact time correction is one line:

τ = −(u·u′ + v·v′) / n²      # hours
t ← t + τ
Why "jump"?
A naive method would nudge t by a tiny step and recompute over and over. This formula instead leaps straight to the exact closest-approach time — if the Moon moved in a perfect straight line. It does not quite, so the leap lands very close but not perfectly, and we repeat. Each repeat gets closer.

The book's stopping rule is |τ| < 0.000001 hours (about 3.6 milliseconds). Watch it converge:

pass 1: t = 0         → τ = +0.458369 h
pass 2: t = 0.458369  → τ = −0.002717 h
pass 3: t = 0.455652  → τ = −0.000043 h
pass 4: t = 0.455609  → τ = −0.000001 h   # |τ| < 0.000001, stop
final:  t = 0.455608 h

So the closest moment is To + 0.455608 h = 10 h 27 m 20 s TD. Subtract ΔT = 65 s to get UT: 10 h 26 m 15 s UT. That is what "jump to the instant of closest approach" means — one leap, then a few tiny corrections to mop up the curvature.

6Step 4 — the answers

At the closest instant, read off the four published quantities.

# Meeus, printed pp. 225-226. k = 0.272495 for a star.
tan P = u / v                                 (4)  position angle on the Moon's disk
Δ     = (u·v′ − v·u′) / (n·k)                 (6)  least separation, in lunar radii
limb clearance = |Δ| − 1
sin h = sin d·sin φ + cos d·cos φ·cos H       (8)  altitude of the star

Plugging in Example 5's final-iteration numbers

# u = −0.13107, v = −0.28541, u′ = +0.40408, v′ = −0.18556, n = 0.444655, k = 0.272495
Δ = (−0.13107 × −0.18556 − (−0.28541) × 0.40408) / (0.444655 × 0.272495)
  = +1.1526 lunar radii        # |Δ| > 1  →  NOT occulted
limb clearance = |Δ| − 1 = 0.1526 lunar radii ≈ 2 arc-minutes

P = 24°.67   (formula 4)   # where on the limb the star would vanish
h = +43°     (formula 8)   # positive → the conjunction is visible from Palomar

The verdict. Because |Δ| > 1, Regulus is not occulted at Palomar. It passes about 0.15 lunar radii — roughly 2 arc-minutes — north of the Moon's limb. The event is still visible (the star is above the horizon, h = +43°); it just is not hidden.

9Why the code is built this way

Why so many files and layers, instead of a few functions in one script?

LayerFileWhy it is separate
Presentationcli.pyParsing, formatting, exit codes. Contains no astronomy.
I/O / adaptersio/elements_file.py
io/location_file.py		Turn JSON/TOML into domain objects, so the source of the elements can change without touching the maths.
Domain modeldomain/occultation.py
domain/observer.py		Plain data with units in the names (reference_hour_td, longitude_deg_east). Validation lives here.
Custom corecore/local_circumstances.py
core/observer_coordinates.py		The textbook formulas. Never imports occultation.reference.
Reference enginereference/skyfield_engine.pyA deliberately empty seam that raises ReferenceEngineUnavailable.

Why not "a few functions in one file"?

  • The reference/custom boundary is the whole point. The plan is to compare this custom implementation against a trusted engine (Skyfield), find disagreements, and only then trust it. In one file you could not tell which numbers came from the textbook and which from Skyfield — and the handoff forbids "claiming independent calculation while still routing the custom engine to Skyfield internally". A test even asserts that importing the reference package does not pull Skyfield into the custom core.
  • Units and time scales are enforced by types, not comments. reference_hour_td (Dynamical Time), longitude_deg_east (east-positive), separation_in_moon_radii. A single-file script would pass raw floats around and someone would eventually feed a west-positive longitude into an east-positive formula.
  • Testability. Because the maths is a pure function over domain values, the regression test can build the inputs from a fixture and assert each output against the printed digits, with a per-quantity tolerance justified by how many digits the book prints.
  • The "smallest testable next increment" discipline. The current increment is exactly "given the elements, find the closest approach". Generating the elements from an ephemeris is a later milestone with its own files. Layering keeps each milestone from leaking into the next.

The trade-off, stated honestly. This is more files and more indirection than a student's first version needs. For a one-off calculation, a single script is fine. The layering only pays off because this project has a long roadmap (a whole Hong Kong almanac) and an explicit rule that the custom core must stay provable and separate from the reference engine. The handoff even warns: "Do not add infrastructure only to make the stack look larger."
